# ByteDance - AML-Production Engineer Intern / Network Software Development Engineer Intern (Network Observation)

## Prompt
Self-introduction (free form, no limit, covering both roles)

## Response
I'm a sophomore at Carnegie Mellon studying computer science, with concentrations in machine learning and security, and most of what I've built has had to keep working after I finished building it.

The clearest version of that is ScottyLabs, where I manage the DevOps infrastructure for a student organization whose deployed services reach over 3,000 users. The job has forced a particular kind of thinking: when a virtual machine goes down and everything running on it disappears, the instinct can't be to find a workaround and move on. You trace what broke, you restore it, and then you figure out the structural change that prevents the next one. Over the past year I've set up and maintained a Matrix homeserver with custom-patched bridge software for real-time cross-platform message mirroring, standardized deployment tooling across eight-plus projects so that every one gets reproducible builds and isolated secret management, and contributed to the governance automation system that keeps team membership, permissions, and platform state synchronized across six platforms from a single source of truth. The work is less about writing code and more about understanding a system well enough to know what it does when something goes wrong.

At MaxMyPoint and in the CUPS lab at CyLab, I've been developing the other side of that. Building and running a production LangGraph chatbot for over 56,000 users has taught me that the hardest problems in ML production aren't modeling problems; they're operational ones. What the model is permitted to do, how you detect when its behavior has drifted, and how you constrain a non-deterministic system serving real users every day are questions that require the same root-cause discipline as infrastructure operations, applied to a system whose failure modes are harder to observe. At CyLab, I traced a silent messaging regression to an undocumented upstream rollback that had no changelog entry, rebuilt everything on the correct fork rather than patching around it, and kept the study valid. The pattern in both cases is the same: find the real source of the problem, not the nearest place to stop it.

Both the AML-Production Engineering and Network Observation roles are asking for exactly that combination: the operational rigor to keep distributed systems healthy, and the monitoring instinct to know when they aren't. I'm drawn to ByteDance's scale because the problems I find most interesting only appear at scale, and the skills I've been developing, fast diagnosis, durable fixes, and building the tooling that tells you where to look, are most tested and most useful there.
