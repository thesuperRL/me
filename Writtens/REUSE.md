# REUSE

## Q1 — Academic & Professional Background, Research Interests, and Career Goals

Across my academic and professional experiences, I have been most drawn not just to building systems, but to understanding them. I find joy in understanding why some approaches work, where others fail, and especially how ideas from different fields can be combined to address problems neither can solve individually. I see computer science less as an end in itself and more as a research tool, most powerful at the boundaries between disciplines.

As my interests evolved from making personal tools to conducting scientific investigations, it became clear that research was the natural path forward. My research experience began with an independent study in high school. Combining my longstanding curiosity about AI with my interest in 3D design, I decided to research generative AI in 3D modeling, writing a review that summarized current findings and outlined potential next steps, both technological and ethical. I traced what I imagined to be "chains of research" across different applications and methods, discovering that the field was still new and lacked a comprehensive literature review. I became especially interested in technologies such as NeRFs, which transform geometric intuition into learnable representations of space.

Seeking to continue the thrill of discovery, I conducted research at Google over the next summer under Mar Gonzalez-Franco, head of the Blended Intelligence Research and Devices Lab, aiming to add Bluetooth connectivity between Unity projects and Android devices. I constantly explored the possibilities for low-latency, accurate, and offline interactions between these devices, given the constraints of older devices. Here, I needed to overcome two major challenges: the first was that Unity has no native Bluetooth support, so I had to write a new library to add it. However, to make it function effectively on lower-capability devices where Bluetooth failed repeatedly, I investigated and used Bluetooth LE, which imposed strict packet-size limitations. Inspired by industry-standard packet loss mitigation methods, I developed a solution that transformed an application barely able to handle one device into one capable of reliably managing two simultaneously.

That experience gave me a setting where uncertainties are expected, constraints are interrogated, and progress comes from asking the right questions rather than implementing an obvious solution. I began looking for the same setting the second I entered college. Motivated by my long-standing curiosity about security and privacy, I applied to a CyLab project led by Professor Lorrie Cranor and Dr. Enze Liu, investigating how people typically use and respond to applications that detect SMS-based messaging scams. This project has required me to integrate skills across software development, AI-driven analysis, and system design, while learning how research progresses collaboratively over time. For the first time, I worked not only with my mentor to find solutions, but also built upon the work of a prior REUSE student — inferring their intent from documentation and comments without being able to communicate with them directly.

I am eager to continue this work within REUSE, where I can not only benefit from a focused, mentored research environment but also conduct interdisciplinary exploration and collaboration. I look forward to working simultaneously on these projects with like-minded peers, in a timeframe where I can keep my heart fully in working on research. The opportunity to work on topics at the intersection of artificial intelligence and security is especially compelling to me, as both fields evolve rapidly through innovation and adversarial dynamics. I see REUSE as a critical step for learning, allowing me to develop research depth, rigor, and collaboration skills at this key time in my life. Simultaneously, it also lets me develop the necessary skills and habits to make lasting contributions to research that others can build upon.

## Q2/Q3 — Why You Are Especially Interested in the Selected Projects

The projects I selected reflect my interests in working at the intersection of reasoning, behavior, and applications, and to reveal human interactions and use rather than just produce outputs. For this reason, across my interests in mathematics, usable security, and AI, I am particularly drawn to research that combines technical depth with careful attention to how tools can be used and understood, which has led to my previous work on Generative 3D AI and my current work in CyLab. For these reasons, I am most interested in Automated Geometric Proof, Usable Security and Privacy, as well as AI Identity Verification.

I'm excited by how the project seeking to Automate Geometric Proofs bridges my interests of education and AI within the mathematical field I know best. Geometry has long been my most comfortable field in mathematics, in large part due to my strong spatial reasoning and early exposure to proof-based mathematics through it. However, geometric proofs pose unique challenges that differ fundamentally from those in other areas of mathematics. In my experience teaching students with aphantasia as a TA, tools like this project would have been very useful. I am particularly excited by the opportunity to design visual and structural representations of proofs that externalize student reasoning, making it easier to diagnose misconceptions and provide targeted guidance.

What excites me most is the challenge of making formal reasoning learnable. Geometric proofwriting requires humans to coordinate between visual intuition, logical deduction, and clear descriptions simultaneously, yet these topics are commonly unintuitive to models that understand other forms of proofs. As I've seen in math competition settings, solutions and hints often fail to convey the underlying intuition, so students would likely still struggle to find these steps. As such, I am especially excited to investigate how reasoning can be modeled, cross-examined, and constructively guided rather than forcefully redirected.

My first security class taught me that many security failures stem from mismatches between system design and human expectations rather than faulty defenses. I've observed that social engineering and phishing were among the easiest and most common attacks on people around me. For these reasons, since before I even arrived at CMU, I've been interested in working at the CUPS lab, especially how its work helps users make better decisions without requiring expert knowledge. Having been a research assistant in the CUPS lab under Professor Cranor for these two semesters, I have come to resonate more and more with its core tenet that security improvements must be rooted in how humans interact with them. My current project involves prototyping and iterating on the application users interact with, working closely with my mentor to evaluate and refine its design based on how participants use it in our study.

Because of this experience, I remain highly interested in Usable Privacy and Security research through CUPS. In REUSE, I aim to continue my work at the CUPS lab, examining how the complexities of security and privacy can be brought closer to the average user. I am drawn to the listed projects, such as IoT data explanations, especially for how they examine how users interpret privacy signals, make trust decisions, and respond to security interventions in real contexts. When I return to CUPS at REUSE, I am most excited to study how passwords and their policies could be made both secure and memorable in simple ways, to explore designs that improve both memorability and compliance among non-expert users.

Last but not least, the project about Generative AI's Risks to Identity Verification stands out to me as a fusion of my interests in AI and Security. This project appeals to me as a concrete example of how rapidly advancing AI capabilities disrupt existing security assumptions. Additionally, having investigated Generative 3D modeling AI through my independent study, I've come across many impressive new ways to create realistic 3D deepfakes from very little data, which, beyond being dangerous for perception on social media, could even be used to fool biometric scanners. I look forward to examining how these tools can be used.

Together, these projects span my interests in AI and security, while also reflecting my interest in interdisciplinary, rigorous research grounded in how humans learn, reason, and interact with the products of research. I see REUSE as an ideal environment to deepen my passion, meet others with similar interests, and contribute to research that others can build upon.

## Q4/Q5 — Why You Are Especially Interested in the Selected Research Areas

REUSE and its participating departments align closely with the way I seek and approach research problems. First, I am motivated to create technologies that are genuinely usable by people, as I believe research is strongest when it is grounded in real human needs. Second, I see computer science not as an end in itself, but as a research tool that becomes most powerful at the intersections of fields. I am drawn to interdisciplinary work that combines established ideas in new ways, especially at the intersections of AI, human behavior, and security. These perspectives help me ask research questions grounded in real constraints while considering unconventional connections across fields.

Among specific research fields, my strongest interests, and likely future concentrations, are Artificial Intelligence and Security & Privacy. My interest in the former centers on understanding how learning systems represent the patterns they witness and how they could be used to inform human learning. It deepened when I led AI development at MaxMyPoint, building and fine-tuning a chatbot for hotel-point optimization. Defining the information the chatbot knew and the actions it was permitted to take was both exciting and challenging, and deepened my interest in AI's non-deterministic behavior and output control. These experiences pushed me toward AI research questions about robustness, interpretability, and specialization. I am especially drawn to research that examines how models represent and retain information, and what connections exist between machine learning mechanisms and human learning.

Since I first entered the field of Security and Privacy, I've been drawn to its inherently adversarial nature. Unlike almost all other fields, progress here is shaped by direct competition between people, where advances on one side force innovation on the other, creating urgency and incentivizing improvement in an often-overlooked field. This dynamic creates an environment in which limits are constantly challenged. After pentesting AI assistants in the Grey Swan AI Safety Hackathon at CMU, my interest and concern grew further as I observed that even the simplest attacks could exploit vulnerabilities in these widely used systems. The gap between rapidly advancing AI capabilities and their security must be closed.

As these AI systems become increasingly prevalent in everyday life, I believe security research must become more anticipatory rather than reactive. Understanding how systems fail, how adversaries adapt, and how defenses work requires careful, specific, and sustained research. Security will continue to demand researcher involvement regardless of technological progress, as even the most advanced systems must be continually evaluated, maintained, and protected. For me, security research offers the opportunity to contribute to a field I believe will remain critical, while applying the mathematical tools I've developed through my coursework to the most high-impact problems.

Outside of my research time, I use personal projects to explore how real people use the systems I create, treating each project as a small-scale experiment. In my Arknights Teambuilder personal project, I used web development skills to create a tool that guides new players to Arknights, serving as a visual interface for an algorithm that optimizes combinations among 400+ characters. Here, I not only explored how to build such an algorithm by drawing on existing examples, but also considered how these explorations could be helpful to users, informing the website's refinement. These projects showed me how programming can function as a method of inquiry, revealing what people need and what research could potentially be done about, and prepared me to approach research from a user-centered perspective.

I wish to bridge theory and practice, finding ways to make all forms of research usable, as I believe the ultimate goal of research is to improve human life. When I saw a project that connected my interest in AI, Security, and usability in the CUPS lab here, I was overjoyed. Now, I am integrating AI and security into a user-centered system, extending prior work by a REUSE researcher and aiming to make informed design judgments aligned with the project's research goals. This experience taught me how much collaborative research depends on clear communication.

I am excited to continue working in these research environments that value precise reasoning, collaboration, and interdisciplinary exploration. REUSE is especially well-suited, as the emphasis on mentored research helps me build intuition by letting me ask precise questions and navigate uncertainty, while the like-minded peers I will meet will provide valuable insights and opportunities for collaboration. I see REUSE as a critical step in developing the necessary depth, rigor, and perspective to contribute meaningfully to research that other researchers can extend and rely on.
